# Research a question

**First, read the core `evidence-base`, whole**: its `core.md` is in the skill folder of that name, beside this one. It holds what every read, brief and piece of research shares: read the source not what points at it, established means confirmed, a gap is a result, how a page is fetched and a blocked one handed over, a refusal ends the step, and the places never read unless named. Do not wear `evidence-base` beside this skill; read its file. What follows is this skill's own.

**Confidence in a conclusion reflects the quality, relevance and limits of the evidence, and nothing else.** Not the number of sources, not how tidy the answer is, not how much the report would benefit from certainty.

The finished document follows `write-for-the-reader`.

## 1. Plan before searching

Write down: **the question, in one sentence**, and its scope: time period, geography, population, and the definitions that decide what counts. Then what evidence would answer it, and which sources could hold that evidence.

**Test the question before trusting it**: a term that can be read two ways, a population or period left undefined, an assumption built into the wording, or two questions sharing one sentence. Split or sharpen it before searching.

**Do not change the question because another one is easier to research.** If the question turns out to be the wrong one, say so and ask.

## 2. Find sources, and rank them for this question

Usually, and only when it fits the question: primary sources and official records, original research, authoritative reference, careful secondary analysis, reputable journalism, then community discussion for experience.

- **Read the source, never the snippet.** A search result is a pointer.
- **Fetch a page as the core says**: with a tool that reads robots.txt, never a bare `curl`. A page that will not load is handed over as a link, never reconstructed from what a results page showed.
- **A vendor page is a claim about the vendor.**
- **A source is not good or bad**, it is fit or unfit for a specific claim. For each: authority and provenance, date, method, primary or secondary, conflicts of interest, scope, limits.

## 3. Extract evidence without adding to it

For each finding, record:

```text
CLAIM       what is asserted
EVIDENCE    the data or passage that supports it, quoted or located exactly
SOURCE      where, with a date
SCOPE       who, where and when it applies to
LIMITATION  what it does not establish
CONFIDENCE  high, medium or low, and why
```

**Never strengthen the language** (the core). A study of one population is not generalised to another either.

## 4. Compare, and keep the disagreement

- **Agreement and disagreement both get reported.** Do not average contradictory evidence into a consensus nobody holds, and do not treat source count as proof.
- **Classify a conflict before resolving it**: true contradiction, different scope, different time period, different definition, different method, apparent only, or unresolved. Disagreement does not mean one source is wrong.
- **Correlation is not presented as cause.**
- **Ask whether a key finding stands alone.** Has anyone else found it, has the field moved since, does the method bear the weight? A single study is a finding, not a settled fact.
- **Popularity is not authority**, and a source's interest in its conclusion is weighed, not used to discard it.
- **A gap is a result.** Missing data, weak or old evidence, an untested assumption: write it down. Never fill it with speculation because the report would feel more complete.

## 5. Write it up

Keep four kinds of statement visibly apart:

```text
FINDING         what the evidence establishes
IMPLICATION     what follows directly from it
RECOMMENDATION  what I suggest doing
POSSIBILITY     an explanation or option nobody has established
```

- **Use only the sections the question needs**: question, scope, method, findings, disagreement, limitations, implications.
- **Plain language, without simplifying away uncertainty.** Precision in the noun, plainness in the sentence around it.
- **Every citation must support the claim beside it.** A citation near a claim is not a citation for it.
- **End with what is not known.**

## Stop when it is answered

When the question is answered to the confidence the evidence allows, stop. More sources past that point are thoroughness for its own sake.

## Personal questions

**Research never ranges into personal folders.** If a question is about the person's own life, the task names the file first, and nothing read goes into a document outside that folder unless they asked for it.

## Before it goes back

1. Does the document answer the question that was asked?
2. Is every conclusion as strong as its evidence, and no stronger?
3. Did I read each source, or a summary of it?
4. Are disagreement, limitations and gaps stated, not buried?
5. Does each citation actually support its claim?
